Cântara – water vessel

Museu Etnográfico da Casa da Cultura de Vimioso (Portugal) · MECC.0193 · 3D documentation by Patrícia Cordeiro · October 2026

View on Sketchfab

About the object

This cântara, from the ethnographic collection of the Casa da Cultura de Vimioso, is made of unglazed clay and measures 27 × 24 × 20.5 cm (inventory). It has a rounded body, a narrow neck, a single handle from the rim to the shoulder, and a band of short incised strokes around the shoulder. [Other marks or signs of use – to confirm.]

Its decoration, surface texture and shape suggest that it may have been made by the women potters (louceiras) of Pinela (Bragança). This is a hypothesis based on comparison with Pinela pottery, not a confirmed attribution; it is being reviewed in the museum's inventory work.

Intangible heritage

Pottery know-how. As a clay vessel, the cântara is first of all the product of the know-how of Portuguese pottery. In the terms of the UNESCO 2003 Convention, it belongs to the domain of traditional craftsmanship. If the Pinela attribution is confirmed, the object also documents a women's pottery tradition of the Bragança region.

Uses. The cântara was used to carry and keep drinking water, at home and in the fields, where it is part of the memory of the harvest.

Associated practices. The museum's intangible cultural heritage (ICH) inventory singles out cântaros as special objects: going to the fountain was an everyday practice associated with women, linked to fountain songs and to social life in the village.

Community voice

In an interview recorded for the museum on 8 June 2026, basket maker Ema Vila-Chã (79, Vale de Pena) remembered her own clay cântaro, more than 80 years old. During the harvest it rolled down a slope from the shade of a cork oak, and reached the path without a single crack. She also explained that woven rings (rodelas) were made to rest the cântaro and the large cooking pots on.

Her story is about a cântaro of her own, not this object; it shows the everyday uses and memories that objects like this one carry.

What I did

I captured, processed and published this model myself, in three photogrammetric captures with an iPad (KIRI Engine). A ruler placed in the scene gave the scale (height 27.3 cm), and turning the object over recorded its base. I oriented, scaled and checked the model in Blender and published it on Sketchfab. Next step, in progress: an annotated scene in Smithsonian Voyager Story. The process is documented in the paradata record, and the metadata follows the Europeana Data Model.
